Fire extinguisher colour chart: every band, every fire

The 36th Company·5 min read·Updated Jul 2026

The fastest way to get it right in an emergency is to recognise the band on sight. Here's the full UK colour chart — every band, what's inside, and the fires it's for — in one scannable place.

The rules behind this
Fire Safety Order 2005 · Art. 13BS EN 3 / BS EN 2BS 5306-8

Every UK fire extinguisher has a red body with a coloured band showing what's inside. This chart maps each band to its extinguisher, the fires it's made for, and — just as important — what you must never use it on.

BandExtinguisherUse onNever use on
RedWaterWood, paper, textiles (Class A)Cooking oil, flammable liquids, live electrics
CreamFoam (AFFF)Class A + flammable liquids (Class B)Cooking-oil fires, live electrics
BlackCO2Electrical equipment + flammable liquids (Class B)Class A (little cooling — can reignite); avoid confined spaces
BlueDry PowderClasses A, B, C and safe near electricsIndoors/occupied rooms (wrecks visibility); cooking oil
YellowWet ChemicalCooking oils & fats (Class F)Not a general-purpose unit — the kitchen specialist
WhiteWater MistMulti-class: A, F, and safe near electricsFollow the maker's rating for liquid fires

The golden rules the chart is built on

  • Water and foam never go on cooking oil or live electrics. Water on hot oil throws a fireball; on electrics it conducts.
  • There is no "Class E." Electrical suitability is shown by a spark pictogram, not a letter — CO2 and powder are the usual choices.
  • CO2 doesn't cool, so a fire can reignite once the gas disperses. Powder is versatile but blinding indoors. Wet chemical is the one for the fryer.

How to use the chart

In a real incident you won't read labels — you'll recognise the band and the pictogram. That recognition, under pressure, is a trained reflex, not knowledge you look up. Print the chart, put it by the extinguishers, and drill it.
