Fire extinguisher colours and chart: what each band means

The 36th Company·7 min read·Updated Oct 2026

Grab the wrong extinguisher and you can make a fire worse. Water on a fryer, or on live electrics, turns a manageable incident into a dangerous one. The colours are a code, and once you know it the right choice takes a second.

Quick answer: UK colour bands

Every UK extinguisher has a red body with a coloured band or label showing its type:
🔴 Red band = Water · 🟡 Cream band = Foam · ⚫ Black band = CO2 · 🔵 Blue band = Dry powder · 🟨 Yellow band = Wet chemical (cooking oil and fat)

The rules behind this
Fire Safety Order 2005 · Art. 13BS EN 3 / BS EN 2BS 5306-10:2019BS 5306-8

Click through to the guide for each band for how it works, how to use it and the mistakes to avoid. Water mist extinguishers are water-based with a finer spray. The colour used for them varies between models, so read the label and the rating rather than relying on the band.

Why they are all red now

Before 1997, UK extinguishers were colour-coded across the whole body, a black CO2 or a blue powder for example. Today, under BS EN 3, every extinguisher body is red, with a second colour covering roughly 5 to 10 per cent of the surface to show the contents. The colour coding is covered by BS 5306-10:2019, which replaced the older BS 7863. Extinguishers coded to the old standard remain acceptable in service. One red colour makes them easy to spot in smoke, and the band tells you what is inside.

What each colour band means

  • Red band: water. For ordinary combustibles. Never on cooking oil or near live electrics. Red band guide →
  • Cream band: foam. A blanket over flammable liquids, and usable on combustibles. Not for electrics or cooking oil. Cream band guide →
  • Black band: CO2. The clean choice for electrical risks and liquids. It leaves no residue but does not cool, so watch for re-ignition. Black band guide →
  • Blue band: dry powder. Versatile and safe near electrics, but indoors it is a last resort because the cloud ruins visibility and is hard to breathe. Blue band guide →
  • Yellow band: wet chemical. The kitchen specialist. It reacts with burning cooking oil to form a soapy seal. The right choice for deep-fat fryers. Yellow band guide →

Fire classes: match the extinguisher to the fuel

UK fire classes come from BS EN 2. The extinguisher has to suit the class of fuel that is actually present:

  • Class A: solids (wood, paper, fabric)
  • Class B: flammable liquids (petrol, oils, solvents)
  • Class C: flammable gases
  • Class D: metals (specialist powder only)
  • Class F: cooking oils and fats
There is no "Class E" in the UK. Electrical fires are not given a letter. An extinguisher that is safe to use near live electrics carries an electrical spark symbol. CO2 and dry powder are the usual choices; water and foam are not. See the fire triangle and classes of fire for the background.

Getting the mix right

Most premises need more than one type. A typical office runs water or foam for general (Class A) risks plus CO2 for electrical equipment. A commercial kitchen adds wet chemical near the cooking line. The specifics (how many, what rating and where) are set out in BS 5306-8 and driven by your fire risk assessment under Article 9 of the Fire Safety Order.

Placement, in short

Wall-mount extinguishers on brackets, never on the floor, on escape routes and near the hazards they cover, with signage above them in larger buildings. As a rule of thumb, no one should travel more than about 30 m to reach a Class A extinguisher, and a Class B unit sits within around 10 m of its risk. Have them serviced annually by a competent person.

The one habit worth drilling

In a real incident there is no time to read the label carefully. The colour band and the pictogram should trigger the right choice automatically, which only happens if you have made that choice enough times before. Recognition under a little time pressure turns "I think it's the yellow one" into muscle memory.

Frequently asked questions

Are all fire extinguishers red?

Almost all of them. Under BS EN 3 the body is red, and the coloured band or label shows the type. Older units coded to the previous scheme may still be in service, so check the label.

Which fire extinguisher is used for electrical fires?

CO2 (black band) and dry powder (blue band) are the usual choices near live electrics. Water and foam are not. Look for the electrical symbol on the label, and isolate the power if you can do so safely.

Which fire extinguisher has a yellow label?

Wet chemical, for cooking oil and fat fires. See the yellow band guide.
