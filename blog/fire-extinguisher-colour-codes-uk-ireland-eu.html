Fire extinguisher colour codes: UK, Ireland and the EU compared

The 36th Company·6 min read·Updated Nov 2026

The UK, Ireland and the rest of the EU all use the same fire extinguisher colour code, thanks to a shared European standard. Here is the code, what changed in 1997, and the few places where the rules around it differ.

At a glance

Same code in: UK, Ireland and EU (EN 3)
Body: red
Zone: cream = foam, blue = powder, black = CO₂, yellow = wet chemical, none = water
Differs: servicing standards and laws, not colours

The rules behind this
BS EN 3I.S. EN 3-7BS 5306-10:2019I.S. 291:2015+A1:2022

One standard across Europe

Fire extinguishers in the UK, Ireland and the rest of Europe all follow the same colour system, because it comes from the same European standard. In the UK it is published as BS EN 3, in Ireland as I.S. EN 3-7, and across the EU as EN 3-7, adopted by each country's standards body. The identification rules are the same: a red body with a small coloured zone to show what is inside.

The colours

  • Water: all red, with no other colour zone. Class A.
  • Foam: red with a cream zone. Classes A and B.
  • Dry powder: red with a blue zone. Classes A, B and C, depending on the powder, and suitable for electrical equipment.
  • Carbon dioxide (CO₂): red with a black zone. Class B and electrical.
  • Wet chemical: red with a yellow zone. Class F, cooking oils and fats.

The zone is a small part of the body, roughly 5 to 10 per cent of the surface depending on the source, normally above the operating instructions on the label. Water mist extinguishers vary by model, so read the label. The full explanation of each is in our fire extinguisher colours and chart, with a page for each band: red, cream, blue, black and yellow.

What changed in 1997

Before 1997, the whole body of an extinguisher was painted in the colour of its contents: blue for powder, black for CO₂, cream for foam. When the current standard took over, extinguishers moved to a red body with a coloured zone. The reasons usually given are that red is easier to see in smoke and that a single body colour is simpler for people who move between countries.

Old extinguishers still in use

Some premises still have extinguishers with a fully coloured body. If you find one, it's a sign of an old unit. Don't assume it is fine because it looks intact:

  • Check the service tag and the date of manufacture on the cylinder or label.
  • Ask your service company whether it can still be serviced and whether replacement is advised.
  • Remove any halon extinguisher. Halon has been phased out and units must be disposed of through the proper route.

See how often fire extinguishers should be serviced for the schedule.

Where the UK and Ireland differ

The colours are the same, but the rules around them are not:

  • Servicing and siting standard: the UK works to BS 5306-3 and BS 5306-8. Ireland uses I.S. 291, revised in 2015 and amended in 2022, which also expects annual servicing by a competent person.
  • Law: in England and Wales the Regulatory Reform (Fire Safety) Order 2005 puts the duty on the responsible person. Scotland and Northern Ireland have their own equivalents. In Ireland the duty comes from the Fire Services Acts and workplace health and safety law.
  • Emergency numbers: 999 or 112 in the UK, 112 or 999 in Ireland.

If you operate in more than one country, work from the standard and law of the country where each building is.

Outside Europe

Don't assume the European colour code applies elsewhere. In the United States, for example, extinguishers are mostly red whatever they contain and are identified by label and pictogram, with classes A, B, C, D and K. Our US extinguisher classes guide explains the difference. Other countries have their own national standards, so check the local one.

Don't rely on colour alone

The colour is a quick way to tell the type of extinguisher at a glance. It is not enough on its own. Before using one, check:

  • The label and pictograms, which show the fire classes it is suitable for and whether it is safe on electrical equipment.
  • The rating, such as 13A or 89B, which shows its capacity on class A and B fires.
  • That you have been trained, the fire is small, the alarm has been raised and your exit is clear.

Frequently asked questions

Do Scotland and Northern Ireland use the same colours as England?

Yes. The colour code comes from the standard, which applies across the UK.

Is a red extinguisher with no coloured zone always water?

Water and water-additive extinguishers are all red. But a red extinguisher that has a different zone, or a label that says otherwise, is not water. Always read the label.

Why does my extinguisher have a different colour from the chart?

It may be an older fully coloured unit, a water mist or specialist model, or from outside Europe. Check the label and ask your service company.
