Fire safety induction for new starters: a checklist you can use on day one

The 36th Company·6 min read·Updated Nov 2026

The Fire Safety Order requires fire safety training when someone is first employed. Here's a ten-minute checklist for day one, who tends to be missed, and how to record it.

At a glance

Law: training when first employed (Art. 21), in working hours
On day one: alarm, exits, assembly point, call points, fire doors, wardens, extinguishers, housekeeping, special arrangements, who to tell
Don't miss: agency staff, contractors, night shift, volunteers
Record: name, date, trainer, topics, signature

The rules behind this
Fire Safety Order 2005 · Art. 21Fire Safety Order 2005 · Art. 19

Why induction is a legal duty

Article 21 of the Fire Safety Order says the responsible person must make sure employees are provided with adequate safety training at the time when they are first employed, and again when they are exposed to new or increased risks. That includes being transferred or given a change of responsibilities, or the introduction of new work equipment or new technology or systems of work. Merseyside Fire & Rescue Service adds that the training must be provided during working hours. So a new starter can't be left to pick it up. They need it at the start.

Under the Order, the training must be repeated periodically where appropriate, adapted to take account of new or changed risks, and provided in a manner appropriate to the risks found in your fire risk assessment. Induction is the first step of a longer cycle, set out in our guide to how often fire safety training is needed.

The ten-minute day-one checklist

A good induction fits into a short walk round the building. Cover these, in this order:

  1. What the alarm sounds like. Let them hear it, or play a recording if you can't test it. State plainly that when it sounds, you leave. It's not a test unless you've been told so.
  2. Their nearest two exits from their desk or workstation, and the route to the final exit. Walk it with them.
  3. Where to go next. Show them the assembly point and explain that no one goes back in until told it is safe. See assembly points and roll call.
  4. How to raise the alarm. Show them the nearest call point, and the number to ring once outside.
  5. Fire doors. They must be kept shut, never wedged open, and should not be blocked. See fire door checks.
  6. Who the fire wardens are. Introduce them, and explain that new starters follow their instructions during an evacuation.
  7. Extinguisher and fire blanket locations, with a clear message: only use one if you have been trained, the fire is small, the alarm has been raised, and you have a clear escape route.
  8. Housekeeping rules: keep escape routes clear, no storage in corridors or stairwells, keep rubbish out of plant rooms, report damaged cables or overloaded sockets.
  9. Special arrangements, including lifts (don't use them), visitors they are responsible for, and any PEEP they or a colleague has. See what is a PEEP.
  10. Who to tell if they see a problem, such as a blocked exit, a propped door or a dead exit sign.

Don't forget the people who aren't on the payroll

Article 21 is about employees, but the people working in your premises who aren't your employees must also be given information about your fire procedures. That means agency and temporary staff, contractors, volunteers, work experience students, and anyone working a different shift pattern. The simplest approach is to treat all of them as new starters.

  • Night and weekend workers are often missed because induction happens in office hours. Arrange a slot for them.
  • Part-timers and job-sharers need the same induction, and should be told who covers their area.
  • Remote and hybrid staff who visit the office occasionally still need to know what to do when they are there.
  • Contractors should be briefed before work starts, particularly if hot work or anything that affects escape routes is planned.

Recording it

Enforcement guidance says you should record the training where you employ five or more people. In any case, keep a simple record: name, date, who delivered it, what was covered, and the new starter's signature. Add it to your fire safety log book, so you can show an inspector or insurer exactly who has been inducted and when.

Making it stick

People forget a short briefing quickly. Three things help:

  • Walk it, don't just tell it. People remember the route they have actually taken.
  • Follow up in the first month. Ask them where their nearest exit is, and where the assembly point is. Correct it gently.
  • Include them in the next drill and tell wardens who the new starters are. See fire drills.
  • Add practice. A short game or quiz that asks them to spot hazards and choose the right action reinforces what they were told.

When to repeat it

Run a fresh briefing whenever someone changes role, you introduce new equipment, work processes or technology, the layout or use of the building changes, or your fire risk assessment is updated in a way that affects them. Cheshire Fire & Rescue Service gives the same examples: a significant change in organisational structure, induction of new equipment or systems of work, and the implementation of new technology.

Frequently asked questions

Does fire induction have to be face to face?

No, but it must be adequate for your risks and include the site-specific information that only a walk-round gives you: exits, assembly point, call points. Online training can cover the general knowledge, but the site briefing still needs to happen.

Does it have to be done on the first day?

The Order says at the time when employees are first employed. The sensible reading is on the first day or as part of the first shift, certainly before they are left alone in the building.

Who should deliver it?

A competent person who knows the building, such as a fire warden, the manager or the person responsible for fire safety. Training should be given by someone competent to deliver it.
