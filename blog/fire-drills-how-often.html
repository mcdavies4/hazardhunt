How often should fire drills be carried out?

The 36th Company·6 min read·Updated Oct 2026

The legal answer is refreshingly simple and slightly annoying: as often as is appropriate to the risk. Here's what that actually means for your building.

The rules behind this
Fire Safety Order 2005 · Art. 15GOV.UK guidanceBS 9999

The baseline: at least once a year

UK Government guidance is clear on the floor: you should carry out at least one fire drill per year and record the results, kept as part of your fire safety and evacuation plan. For a small, low-risk office or shop, that annual drill is the minimum that keeps you on the right side of the law.

Why the law won't give you a number

Article 15 of the Fire Safety Order requires you to establish evacuation procedures and practise them — but deliberately doesn't fix a frequency. Instead it's risk-based: the drill schedule has to reflect your premises, your people and your fire risk assessment. That's why "once a year" is a floor, not a target.

How often, by premises type

  • Low-risk offices & shops — at least annually; every six months is common good practice so new starters and shift workers get a turn.
  • Schools & colleges — typically once a term (with the constant turnover of new pupils and staff).
  • Care homes & hospitals — more often, often quarterly, with extra rehearsal of assisted evacuation and night-time scenarios where staffing is lower.
  • Manufacturing, warehousing & flammable-store risks — frequently monthly, driven by the risk assessment.
Drill immediately if…

You change the layout, finish a refurbishment, update the alarm system, or a previous drill went badly. A new building means the old plan is unproven.

Make the drill actually test something

A drill everyone expects, at the same time every year, teaches people to expect it — which tests nothing. Vary the day and time, keep the wider team in the dark, and change the scenario: block a main staircase to force the secondary route, and watch whether your wardens know their sweep zones. Test any PEEPs and the staff trained to support them.

Record it — or it didn't happen

An inspector will ask for your logbook. For each drill, record the date and time, how long the full evacuation took, any problems (a stiff fire door, an alarm someone couldn't hear), and the actions you took to fix them. Keep the records on file.

Why it's worth the hassle

In a real fire people don't rise to the occasion — they fall back on what they've practised. The single biggest factor in surviving a fire is how quickly everyone leaves, and a rehearsed evacuation is dramatically faster than an improvised one. The drill is where that speed is built.

How to run a fire drill, step by step

  1. Plan it. Decide the scenario (for example, the main stairs blocked), who will observe, and who needs advance notice. Tell the alarm receiving centre first if your alarm is monitored, so the fire service isn't called.
  2. Brief your observers. Give each a stopwatch and a job: one at the assembly point, one at each exit, one watching the slowest route.
  3. Sound the alarm. Use the real alarm. Note the time it started.
  4. Watch what people actually do. Do they stop to collect belongings, use the lift, or head for the door they came in by? Do fire doors close behind them?
  5. Take the roll call. Time how long it takes to account for everyone. See assembly points and roll call.
  6. Stand down and debrief. Reset the alarm, check nobody is left inside, then talk it through while it is fresh.
  7. Record and act. Log the results and assign actions with owners and dates.

What to measure

There is no universal target time, because it depends on the building. What matters is that you record your own times and see them improve or hold steady. Useful measures include: time from alarm to the last person at the assembly point, time to complete the roll call, whether every route you intended to test was used, and how many people needed prompting.

A drill record you can copy

  • Date, time and type of drill (announced or unannounced).
  • Scenario tested, and weather or conditions if relevant.
  • Number of people evacuated, and visitors or contractors included.
  • Time to evacuate, and time to complete the roll call.
  • Problems found: blocked routes, doors, alarm audibility, confusion over the assembly point.
  • Actions agreed, who owns them, and the date they were closed.

Problems drills commonly reveal

  • Staff can't hear the alarm in some rooms, such as stores or toilets.
  • Fire doors that don't close, or exit doors that stick.
  • Nobody knows who is responsible for visitors and contractors.
  • Staff assume someone else will check the toilets.
  • The assembly point is too near the building or blocks the fire service.
  • People who need assistance have no plan. See PEEPs.

Who should be involved

Everyone who is normally in the building: all shifts, part-timers, and temporary staff. Include visitors if you can do so safely. Your fire wardens should have a real task to do, such as sweeping their zone and reporting back. Drills sit alongside, rather than replace, regular fire safety training.

Frequently asked questions

Do drills have to be a surprise?

Not always. Announced drills are useful for new procedures; unannounced ones better reflect reality. A mix works well. Make sure no one is put at risk, for example during a medical procedure or when someone is working at height.

Do I have to tell the fire brigade?

You don't have to tell the fire service about a drill, but if your alarm is monitored by an alarm receiving centre, tell them beforehand so a call-out isn't triggered.

What if someone refuses to take part?

Treat it as a training and management issue: explain why, and record it. Employees have a duty to cooperate on fire safety.
