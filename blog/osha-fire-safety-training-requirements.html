OSHA fire safety training requirements for employers

The 36th Company·6 min read·Updated Nov 2026

OSHA doesn't have one fire training rule. What you must provide depends on whether employees are expected to fight small fires or just evacuate. This guide explains the choices and the annual cycle.

At a glance

If extinguishers are provided for use: education at hire and at least annually
Designated users: equipment training at assignment and annually
Total evacuation policy: no extinguisher training, but an emergency action plan and evacuation training
Also: emergency action plan (1910.38) and fire prevention plan (1910.39)

The rules behind this

The short answer

OSHA doesn't have a single "fire training" rule. It has several, and which ones apply depends on your fire plan. The rule most employers need to know is 29 CFR 1910.157(g), for workplaces that provide portable fire extinguishers for employees to use. Alongside it sit 1910.38 (emergency action plans) and 1910.39 (fire prevention plans).

Step 1: do you want employees to fight fires?

Under 1910.157, you choose a policy, and the choice sets your training duties. OSHA's incipient stage fire means a small fire at its early stage, roughly wastebasket sized or smaller, that can be controlled with an extinguisher.

  • Option 1: total evacuation. If your written fire policy requires everyone to evacuate immediately when the alarm sounds, and nobody is expected to use extinguishers, you need an emergency action plan and a fire prevention plan, and you must train employees to evacuate. You don't have to give extinguisher training.
  • Option 2: designated employees. Only specific employees are expected to fight small fires. They must be trained on the equipment, and everyone else evacuates.
  • Option 3: all employees. Everyone is allowed to use extinguishers, so everyone must be trained.

Step 2: what 1910.157(g) requires

  • Education for all employees where extinguishers are provided for their use: an educational program on the general principles of extinguisher use and the hazards of incipient stage firefighting. It must be given upon initial employment and at least annually thereafter.
  • Training for designated users: employees designated to use firefighting equipment as part of the emergency action plan must be trained in the use of the equipment, upon initial assignment and at least annually thereafter.

Live fire practice isn't mandated. OSHA has said that hands-on experience with actual fires in a controlled environment is not required in a given case. But hands-on practice with a real extinguisher is widely recommended, because people who have only watched a video often freeze.

Step 3: emergency action plan training (1910.38)

An employer with an emergency action plan must designate and train employees to assist in a safe and orderly evacuation of other employees. The employer should also review the plan with each employee on initial assignment, so they know the parts they need to protect themselves. The plan has to cover how to report a fire, evacuation procedures and exit assignments, and how to account for employees afterwards. Employers with 10 or fewer employees may communicate the plan orally.

Step 4: fire prevention plan (1910.39)

A fire prevention plan lists the major workplace fire hazards, how they are stored and handled, potential ignition sources and their controls, and the people responsible for maintaining equipment. Employees must be told about the fire hazards of materials and processes they work with, and the parts of the plan they need to know on initial assignment.

A simple annual plan

  1. At hire: alarm, exits, assembly point, who is in charge, the extinguisher briefing if applicable.
  2. Every year: refresher on extinguisher principles and hazards for all, and equipment training for designated users.
  3. When things change: new plan, new hazards, new duties, new layout.
  4. Each month: visual inspection of extinguishers. Each year: maintenance check.
  5. Drills: practise the evacuation, and time it.
  6. Records: OSHA doesn't prescribe a form for every course, but keeping dated attendance records is how you prove it happened.

Don't forget the state and local rules

Federal OSHA sets the minimum. Many states run their own OSHA-approved plans, which can be stricter, and local fire codes (often based on NFPA standards) add their own requirements for extinguisher selection, signage, inspection and drills. Check with your state OSHA plan and your fire marshal.

How this compares with the UK

In Britain, the Fire Safety Order requires fire safety training when staff are first employed, and repeated periodically where appropriate, without a fixed annual interval. See how often fire safety training is needed in the UK. For the five US extinguisher classes, see our US classes guide.

Frequently asked questions

Is fire extinguisher training mandatory?

If you provide extinguishers for employees to use, yes: education at hire and annually. If you require total evacuation and employees aren't expected to fight fires, you aren't required to give extinguisher training.

Does the training have to be hands-on?

The rule requires an educational program, and training in equipment use for designated employees. It doesn't mandate live-fire practice, but hands-on practice is the best way to make people confident and competent.

How often is the refresher?

At least annually, for both the general education and the designated-user training.
