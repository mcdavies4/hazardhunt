Fire safety log book: what to record, and a template you can copy

The 36th Company·7 min read·Updated Nov 2026

A fire safety log book is the single record that shows your fire precautions are being tested, maintained and practised. Here is what to put in it, how often to fill it in, and where to download a free template.

At a glance

What it is: one record of tests, checks, servicing, training and drills
Required by name? No, but fire services expect records
Core sections: alarm, emergency lighting, extinguishers, fire doors, drills, training, risk assessment
Golden rule: every check dated, signed, with faults and fixes recorded

What a fire safety log book is for

A fire safety log book is one place where you record everything that shows your fire precautions are working: tests, checks, servicing, training, drills and visits by inspectors. The Fire Safety Order doesn't use the term, but fire and rescue services across the UK publish log book templates, because records are the easiest way to show that you are doing what your fire risk assessment says. Avon Fire & Rescue Service puts it this way: keeping a record of checks, tests and maintenance demonstrates due diligence and compliance with the Order.

A good log book also helps you. It shows what is due next, who did the last check, and which fault has been open for three weeks.

What to put in it

Most fire service log books follow the same layout. Use these sections as your starting point:

  1. Premises details and key contacts: address, responsible person, fire wardens, and the alarm, extinguisher and emergency lighting contractors.
  2. Fire risk assessment: date, who carried it out, date of the last review and the next review date. See the small business fire risk assessment guide.
  3. Fire alarm: weekly test records, six-monthly or other servicing by an engineer, and faults. See the alarm systems guide.
  4. False alarms: date, time, cause and action taken.
  5. Emergency lighting: monthly and annual tests. See emergency lighting testing.
  6. Fire extinguishers: monthly checks, annual service and any recharges. See how often to service extinguishers.
  7. Fire doors and escape routes: routine checks of exits, doors, seals and signs. See fire door checks.
  8. Fire drills: date, time to evacuate, problems found and actions. See how often to hold fire drills.
  9. Staff training: who, when, what was covered and who delivered it. See how often staff need fire training.
  10. Visits and inspections: by the fire service, insurers or auditors, and what they recommended.
  11. Special equipment: sprinklers, hose reels, smoke control, hot work permits and anything else particular to your premises.

How often to record each check

  • Daily: a walk through escape routes, fire exits and doors before opening. Record problems, or a daily tick sheet if you prefer.
  • Weekly: fire alarm test (a different call point each week), a look at fire doors and exit signs.
  • Monthly: extinguisher visual checks and emergency lighting flick test.
  • Six-monthly or quarterly: alarm service by an engineer, as set by your system's schedule.
  • Annually: extinguisher service, emergency lighting full-duration test, fire risk assessment review, refresher training for staff.
  • Each time: drills, new starters' induction training, false alarms and fire service visits.

A layout you can copy

You don't need a special product. A spreadsheet, a notebook or a shared document works, as long as the entries are dated, signed and kept. Use one page or tab per record type, with these columns:

  • Fire alarm test: date, call point or zone tested, result (pass/fail), tested by, fault and action, date fixed.
  • Extinguisher check: date, location, type, in place and unobstructed (yes/no), pin and seal intact (yes/no), gauge in green (yes/no), damage noted, checked by.
  • Emergency lighting test: date, monthly or annual, fittings tested, fittings failed, repair raised (reference), tested by.
  • Fire door and exit check: date, door or exit, closes fully, clear of obstruction, signs visible, issue found, action and date closed.
  • Drill record: date, time, announced or not, evacuation time, roll call time, issues found, actions and owners.
  • Training record: date, name, induction or refresher, topics covered, trainer, signature.
  • Actions log: what, who owns it, priority, due date, date closed. Every failed check goes here.

Write the date a fault is fixed as well as the date it was found. North Yorkshire Fire & Rescue's log book makes the same point: record every check, test and maintenance task, including faults and the remedial action taken.

Free log books from UK fire services

Several fire and rescue services publish free log books you can download and fill in. They are written for UK premises and are the best starting point if you want a ready-made format:

  • Cambridgeshire Fire & Rescue: log book template for simple premises
  • Avon Fire & Rescue: log book for non-domestic premises
  • Cheshire Fire & Rescue: log book (Word document)
  • Leicestershire Fire & Rescue: log book

Use the one that matches your type of premises, and adapt it. The log book is a tool for your premises, not a form for its own sake.

Keeping it useful

  • Keep it where the people doing the checks can reach it, and tell the fire service and your insurer where it is. If it is digital, make sure at least two people can open it.
  • Name a person who owns it. If everyone is responsible, nobody is.
  • Review it monthly. Look for gaps, repeated faults and overdue actions.
  • Keep old records. Ask your insurer how long they expect, and keep records at least until the next review of your risk assessment.
  • Don't tick without looking. A record that says a check was done when it wasn't is worse than a gap. Be honest and note what you couldn't check.

Frequently asked questions

Is a fire safety log book a legal requirement?

The Fire Safety Order doesn't require a book with that name. It does require you to maintain your fire precautions and, in many premises, to record your fire risk assessment. A log book is the standard way to evidence this, and fire inspectors will ask what records you keep.

Paper or digital?

Either. What matters is that entries are dated and attributable, can't be quietly changed, and can be produced when asked. Digital makes reminders and reports easier. Paper is simpler for daily walk-rounds.

How do I record staff training?
