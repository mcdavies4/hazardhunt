Which fire extinguisher for which fire?

The 36th Company·5 min read·Updated Oct 2026

You don't fight a chip-pan fire the way you fight a burning bin. The right extinguisher depends on what's actually burning — the fire's class. Here's the class-by-class guide.

The rules behind this
Fire Safety Order 2005 · Art. 13BS EN 3 / BS EN 2BS 5306-8

Fires are grouped by what's burning — their class (BS EN 2; see the fire triangle and classes of fire for why). The right extinguisher matches the class; the wrong one can make things violently worse. Here's the class-by-class rundown.

Class A — wood, paper, textiles

The everyday fire. Use water, foam, or water mist — they cool the fuel below its ignition point.

Class B — flammable liquids (petrol, oil, solvents)

Use foam (blankets the surface), CO2, or dry powder. Never water — it spreads burning liquid.

Class C — flammable gases

The first action is to isolate the gas supply if it's safe to do so. Dry powder is the extinguisher for gas fires — but a burning gas leak is usually a job for the fire service, not a first-aid extinguisher.

Class D — metals

Burning metals (magnesium, swarf) need a specialist powder made for the metal — ordinary ABC powder won't do. Rare outside industry.

Electrical fires — not a class

There's no "Class E." An extinguisher safe near live electrics carries a spark symbol: use CO2 (no residue, kind to equipment) or dry powder. Never water or foam. Cut the power first if you can — then it becomes whatever class the burning material is.

Class F — cooking oils & fats

The chip-pan and deep-fat-fryer fire. Only wet chemical is designed for it — it reacts with the oil to form a sealing, cooling layer. Never throw water on burning oil, and never move a burning pan.

Quick decision

Paper/wood → water or foam. Petrol/oil-based liquid → foam, CO2 or powder. Live electrics → CO2 or powder. Cooking oil → wet chemical. Gas or metal → isolate/specialist, and think fire service.

Before you pick anything up

Only tackle a small fire, with your back to a clear exit, once the alarm's raised and people are leaving. If it's bigger than a bin, or you're unsure — get out, stay out, call 999. Not sure which colour is which? See the fire extinguisher colour chart.

Dangerous mismatches to avoid

  • Water on a cooking oil or fat fire. The water sinks, flashes to steam and throws burning oil upwards.
  • Water or foam on live electrical equipment. Both conduct electricity.
  • Water on a flammable liquid fire. It can float the fuel and spread the fire.
  • Powder in a small, enclosed room. You can lose sight of the exit and breathe the powder in.
  • CO2 on a deep-seated Class A fire. It doesn't cool, so the fire can restart.

Which extinguishers does a workplace actually need?

Your fire risk assessment decides, using BS 5306-8 as guidance. As a common rule of thumb for general (Class A) risks, there should be at least two extinguishers on each floor, with enough rated capacity for the floor area, commonly one 13A-rated unit per 200 square metres. Then add the specialist types for particular risks:

  • Offices and general areas: water or foam for Class A, plus CO2 near computers and electrical equipment.
  • Kitchens: wet chemical for Class F, and a fire blanket.
  • Workshops, garages and stores with flammable liquids: foam, CO2 or powder, as the risk requires.
  • Boiler rooms and plant rooms: usually CO2 or powder.

Where to put them

  • On the escape route, near exits, so people are never fighting a fire with their back to the flames.
  • Together in fire points, so people can find the right type quickly.
  • Wall-mounted or on a stand, with the carrying handle typically about 1.5 metres from the floor for lighter units and lower for heavier ones.
  • With a sign above, and unobstructed by stock or furniture.

Using one safely: the four steps

  1. Pull the safety pin.
  2. Aim at the base of the fire, not the flames.
  3. Squeeze the handle.
  4. Sweep from side to side, and keep watching in case it reignites.

Only do this if the fire is small, the alarm has been raised, and your exit is clear. Get trained first. Our guide to how often staff need fire safety training explains what to cover.

Service and checks

Extinguishers need a basic visual check regularly and an annual service by a competent person, under BS 5306-3. Follow the routine in our weekly, monthly and annual checks guide.

The class table tells you what a type of extinguisher can do. The label on your extinguisher tells you what that specific model is rated for, so always check it. For each colour band in detail see the red, cream, blue, black and yellow band pages, or the overall colour chart.
