Commercial kitchen fire safety: grease, gas, extraction and what to keep

The 36th Company·7 min read·Updated Nov 2026

Kitchen fires usually start with fat and grease, and often spread through the extraction system. Here are the controls insurers and fire officers look for, from TR19 duct cleaning to Class F extinguishers.

At a glance

Extraction: TR19 Grease cleaning, measured, by a specialist
Gas: interlock tied to the ventilation
Suppression: wet chemical system, serviced at least every 6 months
Kit: fire blanket plus a Class F extinguisher
Keep: certificates, service and training records

The rules behind this
Fire Safety Order 2005BESA TR19 GreaseGas Safety Regulations

Why kitchens are different

Commercial kitchens combine open flames, hot oil, gas supplies, electrical equipment running at full load and grease-laden extraction systems. A flare-up at the cooker can reach grease built up in the canopy and ductwork, and a duct fire spreads through the extraction system and into the structure of the building, often out of sight. Your fire risk assessment has to deal with all of this, and insurers and fire inspectors will look at it closely.

1. Extraction and grease: TR19

TR19 Grease is the specification from the Building Engineering Services Association (BESA) for the fire risk management of grease accumulation in kitchen extraction systems. It applies to the whole extract system, from canopy through the ductwork to where it leaves the building. It isn't a statute, but it is widely referenced by insurers, landlords, fire officers and environmental health officers as the benchmark.

  • Cleaning frequency is set by risk. It depends on how much grease your cooking produces, so a busy fryer-heavy kitchen needs cleaning far more often than a light-use one.
  • Measure, don't guess. A wet film thickness gauge is used to measure the grease on the duct surfaces before and after cleaning. Visual inspection alone isn't enough.
  • Access matters. Ductwork needs enough access hatches to clean it properly. Insurers' guidance mentions hatches at intervals of no more than 2 metres.
  • Use a specialist. This is not a job for the kitchen team. Hire a contractor who works to TR19 and keep their certificates and reports. Fire officers and insurers will ask for them.

2. Gas safety and the interlock

Where gas cooking appliances are used with mechanical extraction, insurers expect a gas interlock: if the ventilation fails, the gas supply is shut off automatically. This stops gas burning in a room that isn't being ventilated. Check that the interlock works, that nobody has bypassed it, and that gas appliances are serviced by a Gas Safe registered engineer.

3. Fire suppression over the cooking line

Insurers commonly expect an automatic wet chemical fire suppression system over commercial cooking ranges and fryers. It should be designed and installed by an approved company, protect the appliances, canopy and ducting, have a manual override, and be serviced at least every six months.

4. Extinguishers and fire blankets

Where deep-fat frying takes place, provide a fire blanket and an annually maintained wet chemical (Class F) extinguisher. Wet chemical extinguishers react with burning oil to form a soapy layer, which seals the surface and stops reignition. Other extinguishers can spread burning oil. The right choice for the rest of the kitchen, including electrical equipment, depends on the risks. See which extinguisher for which fire and how often to service them.

5. Daily habits that prevent fires

  • Clean as you go. Wipe grease from hobs, hoods, filters and around fryers every day.
  • Change and clean canopy filters on schedule.
  • Control the fuel. Keep oil containers, cardboard, cloths and cleaning chemicals away from heat, and take rubbish out regularly.
  • Switch off at the end of service. Turn off the gas and electrical appliances and check them.
  • Look after fryers. Don't overfill, don't leave them unattended when heating, and keep thermostats and cut-outs working.
  • Test electrics. Overloaded sockets and damaged leads are an ignition source, especially in damp conditions.

6. What to teach every kitchen worker

  • Never use water on a fat or oil fire. It sinks, turns to steam and throws burning oil upwards.
  • If it's safe, turn off the heat, and cover the pan with a fire blanket or lid. Don't carry or move it.
  • If the fire grows or you cannot control it, raise the alarm, get out and call 999.
  • Know where the emergency gas and electric shut-offs and the suppression system's manual trigger are.
  • Know the evacuation routes from the kitchen, which should never be blocked by deliveries or bins. See fire safety induction.

What to keep on file

  • Duct cleaning certificates and grease measurement reports.
  • Suppression system service records.
  • Gas safety certificates and interlock test records.
  • Extinguisher and fire blanket checks.
  • Training records for kitchen staff.

Put these in your fire safety log book.

Frequently asked questions

Is TR19 a legal requirement?

Not on its own. The Fire Safety Order requires you to manage your fire risks. TR19 is the recognised standard for the extraction system, so it is what inspectors and insurers expect to see you meeting.

How often should kitchen extraction be cleaned?

It depends on how much grease your cooking produces, which is why the frequency should come from a risk assessment and measurements, not a calendar.

Do small cafés need all of this?

If you have a canopy and extraction duct, the principle applies at any size. Scale the measures to the risk, and ask your insurer what they require.
