How many fire wardens do I need? A practical guide for UK workplaces

The 36th Company·6 min read·Updated Nov 2026

There is no legal number of fire wardens. The law says you need enough competent people for your premises and risks. Here's how to work out a sensible number, starting from the common ratios.

At a glance

Law: no fixed number; enough competent people for the premises
Rule of thumb: 1 per 50 (low risk), 1 per 20 (medium), 1 per 15 (high)
Then adjust for: floors, shifts, absence cover, people needing help, layout

The rules behind this
Fire Safety Order 2005 · Art. 18Fire risk assessment

What the law says

The Regulatory Reform (Fire Safety) Order 2005 does not give a number. It requires the responsible person to appoint one or more competent persons to help carry out preventive and protective measures, and to make sure there are enough of them, with the training, time and equipment they need, for the size of the premises and the risks. So the real answer is: as many as your fire risk assessment says you need. The ratios below are rules of thumb that assessors and training providers use to get a first estimate.

The common rule of thumb

Training providers commonly give three ratios, and are clear that they are a rough guide and not set in law:

  • Low risk: one warden per 50 people.
  • Medium risk: one warden per 20 people.
  • High risk: one warden per 15 people.

Most offices, shops and similar workplaces are treated as medium risk. Other guidance gives different figures, for example at least one warden for fewer than 20 employees, two for 20 to 75, and one more for every additional 75. The spread between sources is itself the lesson: use a ratio to get started, then adjust for your building.

Five things that push the number up

  1. Floors and zones. You need someone able to sweep each floor or area. One warden per floor is a common minimum, so a small team on three floors needs at least three wardens on duty.
  2. Shifts and opening hours. Wardens must be available whenever people are in the building: early starts, late shifts, weekends and nights. A warden who works Monday to Wednesday doesn't cover Thursday.
  3. Absence. Holidays, sickness and training courses mean some wardens won't be there. Plan cover so that the minimum is met on a bad day, not just a good one.
  4. People who need help. If anyone needs assistance to leave, for example a wheelchair user, someone with a hearing or visual impairment, or a visitor with limited mobility, you need wardens who can help them. See PEEPs and GEEPs.
  5. Layout and risk. Complex layouts, large floor plates, high-hazard processes and sleeping accommodation all call for more wardens, not fewer. Buildings with many vulnerable occupants, such as care homes, need more than the general ratios.

A worked example

Take an office with 45 staff on two floors, working 8am to 6pm. It's medium risk, and one visitor uses a wheelchair on most days.

  • The ratio gives 45 ÷ 20, so about 3 wardens on duty.
  • Two floors means at least one warden on each, so 3 still works, with one extra able to help the visitor.
  • To cover holidays and sickness, appoint a deputy for each floor, so about 5 or 6 trained wardens in total.

The assessor may adjust this up or down, but the reasoning is the point: ratio, then floors, then people needing help, then absence.

What small businesses should do

  • Under about 10 staff on a single floor, one trained warden and a trained deputy is often enough, if your risk assessment agrees.
  • Where you share a building, agree with the landlord or other tenants who is covering which parts, so no area has none and none are duplicated.
  • Remember the fire marshal role is separate from first aid. Don't assume your first aiders will be on duty at the same time.

Choosing the right people

Good wardens are people who are on site regularly, are willing, can stay calm and can be heard. They should be physically able to walk the area, and they should be released from work to do the role. They also need appropriate training. See our guides to fire warden training and how often training should be refreshed.

Writing it down

Record in your fire risk assessment and log book how many wardens you have, who they are, which area each covers, who their deputies are, and when they were last trained. Update it when people leave or change role. A sample layout is in our fire safety log book guide.

Frequently asked questions

Is there a legal minimum number of fire wardens?

No. The law requires enough competent people for your premises and risks. The ratios are guidance.

Is a fire warden the same as a fire marshal?

In practice, yes. The terms are used interchangeably. Some organisations use marshal for the role in an evacuation and warden for the wider role.

Can one warden cover a whole building?

Only in a very small, simple building. In most premises, one person cannot sweep several floors quickly, and they might be absent.

Do wardens need to be employees?

Not necessarily, but they must be competent and reliably present. A person who is rarely on site is a poor choice.
