Emergency lighting testing: monthly and annual requirements

The 36th Company·7 min read·Updated Nov 2026

Emergency lighting has to work when the power fails, and the only way to know is to test it. In the UK that means a short test every month and a full-duration test once a year, both written down.

At a glance

Monthly: short functional (flick) test
Annually: full-duration test, normally three hours
Daily: indicator check on central battery systems
Always: log every test, fault and repair

The rules behind this
Fire Safety Order 2005 · Art. 14BS 5266-1BS EN 50172 / BS 5266-8

Why it matters

If the power fails in a fire, or the fire itself cuts the lights, people still have to find the way out. Emergency lighting lights the escape route, the exit signs and key points such as stairs, changes of direction and fire equipment. It only helps if it works when it is needed, and batteries in particular deteriorate without anyone noticing. Testing is how you find out in advance.

The law and the standards

The Fire Safety Order 2005 says emergency routes and exits that need emergency lighting must have it, with enough intensity (Article 14). BS 5266-1 is the code of practice for emergency lighting of premises, and BS EN 50172 (also published as BS 5266-8) covers inspection and testing. Your fire risk assessment decides where emergency lighting is needed. The standards describe how to design it, test it and keep it working.

The two tests every system needs

  • Monthly: the functional test. Often called the flick test. It's a short test that simulates a mains failure to check that every light and sign comes on.
  • Annually: the full-duration test. The mains supply is cut for the whole rated duration of the system, usually three hours, to prove the batteries can last. Some systems are designed for one hour, and the test runs for that rated time.

How to do the monthly flick test

  1. Choose a quiet time and tell staff what you are doing. Avoid periods when the building is full or after dark if you can't safely keep the premises lit by normal lighting.
  2. Use the test key or switch provided for the emergency lighting circuit to simulate a power cut. Don't switch off the main fuse board.
  3. Walk the building and check that every emergency light, exit sign and bulkhead comes on. It only takes a short time, enough to see that each fitting is working.
  4. Restore the supply and check that each fitting returns to normal and any "charging" indicator is showing healthy.
  5. Log it: date, who tested, what you tested, and any fittings that failed or looked dirty, damaged or obscured.
  6. Report faults to your maintenance contractor straight away, and record the date each was fixed.

How to do the annual full-duration test

The annual test is more disruptive, and many organisations hire an engineer to carry it out. The main points:

  • Plan it for a time when the building is empty or when normal lighting can stay on in areas people use, because the emergency lights will be draining their batteries.
  • Switch the emergency lighting circuit to battery for the full rated duration, normally three hours, then check that every fitting is still lit at the end of the test.
  • Note any fitting that goes out early. That is a battery failure and the unit needs repair or replacement.
  • Allow the batteries to recharge fully afterwards. Manufacturers typically allow up to 24 hours, so don't schedule the test just before the building will be occupied in the dark.
  • Record the result for every fitting.

Central battery systems and self-testing lights

Some buildings run emergency lighting from a central battery system rather than from batteries in each light. These need an additional daily check of the central unit's indicators. Many modern systems are also self-testing, running automatic monthly and annual tests and reporting faults. Self-test doesn't remove the duty to look: you still need to check the indicators, act on faults, and keep a record.

What to record

  • A schedule or plan showing where every fitting is.
  • The date of every monthly and annual test, and who did it.
  • Which fittings passed and which failed.
  • What repair or replacement was done, and the date.
  • Any changes to the building, such as new partitions, that may need the system to be reviewed.

Keep this with your other fire records. Our fire safety log book guide gives a layout you can copy.

Common problems found at testing

  • Dead batteries. The most common failure, and the reason the annual test exists.
  • Dirty or obscured fittings, covered by stock, banners or a new ceiling tile.
  • Coverage gaps after a layout change, so a corridor or stair has no light.
  • No test record, so there is no evidence of testing even where it was done.
  • Exit signs that don't light when mains power fails. See fire safety signs and what they mean.

Frequently asked questions

Who is allowed to test emergency lighting?

A competent person. A trained member of staff can usually carry out the monthly flick test. The annual full-duration test and any repairs are normally done by a qualified electrician or emergency lighting contractor.

Does a small shop or office need emergency lighting?

Your fire risk assessment decides. If people use the premises when it is dark, or the escape route has no natural light, emergency lighting is likely to be needed. Premises with sleeping accommodation need particular care.

Is emergency lighting the same as exit signs?

No. Exit signs show the way. Emergency lighting lights the route. Many exit signs are internally lit and form part of the emergency lighting system, and they need testing too.

How does this fit with the other fire checks?

Alongside the weekly fire alarm test, monthly extinguisher checks and fire door checks. Our checks schedule puts them all in one place.
